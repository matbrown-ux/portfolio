You can't rank in ChatGPT the way you rank on Google, because there is no list of positions to climb. ChatGPT writes an answer and names a few businesses in it. To get named, your website has to be open to its search crawler, your listings have to agree with your site, and you need a clear reason to be the answer for one specific kind of customer.

## How does ChatGPT decide which local businesses to recommend?

There's a lot of confident advice about this out there, and it gets overwhelming fast. ChatGPT doesn't publish a local ranking formula, so nobody outside OpenAI knows the exact recipe, and anyone who gives you a percentage is guessing. What I can tell you is what the agencies describe and what I saw in a test I ran on a real shop.

Start with the part that is documented. OpenAI's <a href="https://developers.openai.com/docs/bots" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="OpenAI's crawler documentation, opens in a new tab">crawler documentation</a> says OAI-SearchBot "is used to surface websites in search results in ChatGPT's search features," and that sites opted out of it "will not be shown in ChatGPT search answers." So the first gate is plain: if you block that bot, you are out. A separate bot called GPTBot is for training OpenAI's models, and you can allow or block each one on its own in your robots.txt file, the small text file that tells crawlers which pages they may visit. That means you can keep your site out of model training and still show up in ChatGPT search.

Past that gate, the pattern in the guides I read is consistent. A <a href="https://www.bigeyeagency.com/insights/how-do-i-get-my-business-mentioned-in-chatgpt-a-complete-guide-to-ai-search-visibility" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="Bigeye's guide to getting mentioned in ChatGPT, opens in a new tab">guide from the agency Bigeye</a> says ChatGPT favors businesses with consistent information across platforms, strong reviews, clear website content and mentions on reputable sites. Bigeye also says its claims about how ChatGPT ranks businesses are its own interpretation, not documented behavior, and I would read it that way too. <a href="https://www.localfalcon.com/blog/how-to-rank-in-chatgpt-local-search-results" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="Local Falcon's ChatGPT local search guide, opens in a new tab">Local Falcon</a> puts it more bluntly: ChatGPT has no formal local ranking algorithm, and "ranking" just means whether you are mentioned and in what order.

Both of those companies sell tracking or visibility services, so treat them as informed opinions with a stake in the answer. They agree with each other and with what I saw, which is why I use them as a starting point and not as proof.

## What did I see when I tested a real local business?

On October 16, 2026, at about 7:30pm Hawai'i time and logged out, I ran three prompts in Google AI Mode, ChatGPT, Gemini and Perplexity for 'Aiwi Waffles, a waffle shop in Waikiki I do the brand, website and search work for. 'Aiwi appeared in all 12 answers and came first in 8 of them.

The three prompts and the results:

| best waffles in Waikiki | Second in all four |
| best gluten-free breakfast in Honolulu | First in all four |
| where can I get gluten-free waffles on Oahu | First in all four, and Gemini named only 'Aiwi |

The first row is the one I find funniest and most useful. For the broad "best waffles in Waikiki" prompt, another shop took first place in all four tools. That shop, Waffle and Berry, has about 2,900 Google reviews, and 'Aiwi had 214 as of October 2026. A shop with that many reviews wins the generic question, and honestly that makes sense, since a pile of reviews is the loudest thing any tool can read. I'm not going to out-review 2,900 people with a clever paragraph, and neither are you.

The two gluten-free prompts went the other way. 'Aiwi was first in every tool, and Gemini named only 'Aiwi for the Oahu question. The shop is also ranked #1 for gluten-free waffles in Honolulu on Find Me Gluten Free, where it has 182 reviews and a 5.0 rating as of October 2026. Gemini and Perplexity cited aiwiwaffles.com most often, and the other sources that turned up included Yelp, Find Me Gluten Free, Atly, Wanderlog and Grace & Lightness Magazine.

## Where does ChatGPT get local business information?

ChatGPT gets local business information from its training data plus live web searches, and from the places people already list businesses: your own website, directories, review sites and articles written about you. It draws on a wide web of sources, so the work is making those sources agree with each other.

The <a href="https://www.bigeyeagency.com/insights/how-do-i-get-my-business-mentioned-in-chatgpt-a-complete-guide-to-ai-search-visibility" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="Bigeye's guide, opens in a new tab">Bigeye guide</a> says live searches pull from the same kinds of results that power Google and Bing, such as local listings, business websites and reviews. I couldn't find a primary source from OpenAI that confirms which search index sits behind it, so I won't tell you ChatGPT "uses Bing" as if it were settled.

What I can show from my own test is which pages the tools pointed to. The shop's own website, a gluten-free directory, Yelp and a few travel and food sites showed up. That list is a good map of where to spend your time:

## Does my Google Business Profile affect AI answers?

It probably helps indirectly, and nobody outside OpenAI can prove how much. A Google Business Profile is a public listing of your hours, address, category and reviews, so it is one of the places the web agrees on facts about you. The tools that read the web will often see it, but I can't show you a documented link between your profile and a ChatGPT answer.

Google's own tools are a clearer case. Google says on its <a href="https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="Google's AI features and your website page, opens in a new tab">AI features page</a> that to appear in AI Overviews or AI Mode a page "must be indexed and eligible to be shown in Google Search with a snippet," and that there are no additional requirements or special optimizations. It also says you don't need new machine readable files, AI text files or markup. So for Google's AI answers, normal SEO is the job.

For 'Aiwi, the profile work is where the local ranking came from. The case study says I optimized the Google Business Profile and the shop now ranks #1 on Google for multiple local searches, with about 13,000 views a month across Google Search and Maps. I have a whole write-up of that in my <a href="/blog/technical-seo/local-seo-case-study-aiwi-waffles">local SEO case study</a>. I can't tell you how much of the ChatGPT result came from that profile and how much came from the website, and I'd be inventing a number if I tried.

Reviews belong in this section too, and they have a rule. Google's <a href="https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114" target="_blank" rel="noopener" aria-label="Google's review policy, opens in a new tab">contribution policy</a> says merchants may not "selectively solicit positive reviews from customers," and may not pressure people to write reviews while they are on the premises. It allows you to encourage genuine ones. The way 'Aiwi handles it is to talk with guests about their meal, and when someone says it was great, the owner or staff mention that a Google review helps a lot. The ask only follows the customer starting the conversation.

## Do Bing Places and Yelp matter for ChatGPT?

They may, but I can't prove it, and I won't pretend otherwise. Several guides, including the Bigeye one, recommend claiming and matching your profiles on Bing Places, Yelp and other directories. It costs you an afternoon, and the same listings feed other tools too, so it is a cheap bet.

Yelp did show up in the sources the tools cited for 'Aiwi, which fits the advice. I did not test Bing Places on its own, so I have nothing first-hand to say about it. What I'd tell an owner is to treat directories like a checklist of rooms where people might ask about you. Walk into each one, make sure the name, address, phone number and hours are right, and leave. A listing with last year's hours looks fine at a glance and is absolutely atrocious for a tool trying to tell a hungry stranger when you open.

Here is a short table of what each place is for:

| Place | What to check | Why bother |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your website | Name, address, hours, what you are best at, said in plain words | Both Gemini and Perplexity cited the shop's own site most often in my test |
| Google Business Profile | Category, hours, photos, reviews | It drives your Google local ranking, and the web reads it |
| Bing Places | Same details as Google | Recommended by several guides, unproven for ChatGPT |
| Yelp and niche directories | Claimed, complete, matching | They were among the sources the tools cited |

## How do I make sure ChatGPT can read my site?

Don't block OAI-SearchBot, and make the pages you want quoted plain, fast and written in sentences that stand on their own. OpenAI says OAI-SearchBot is the bot to manage in robots.txt for search, and a blocked site won't be shown in ChatGPT search answers.

Open your robots.txt file at yourdomain.com/robots.txt and look for the line that says `User-agent: OAI-SearchBot`. If you see `Disallow: /` under it, the door is shut. A developer or a plugin may have added that rule years ago without telling you, so it is worth thirty seconds to look.

```txt
User-agent: OAI-SearchBot
Allow: /

User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /
```

That example lets ChatGPT search find your pages while keeping them out of model training. OpenAI's documentation says GPTBot is for training and that disallowing it "indicates a site's content should not be used in training generative AI foundation models." Whether you block GPTBot is your call. It doesn't change whether you can appear in search answers.

There is a third bot on that page, ChatGPT-User, which acts when a person asks ChatGPT to visit a page. OpenAI says it isn't used for automatic crawling and isn't used to decide whether content appears in search, and that robots.txt rules may not apply to it. You don't need to do anything about that one.

After the crawler gate, write the way the answer engines read. Put what you do and where you do it in a plain sentence near the top of the page. If you serve gluten-free customers, say so on the page, not just on a menu PDF. A tool can only repeat what it can find in a sentence. ## How do I check if AI recommends my business?

Ask the questions your customers ask, in each tool, and write down who gets named and in what order. That is what I did for 'Aiwi, and it took one evening. Do it logged out, so your own history doesn't tilt the answer.

Here is the method I'd use:

1. Write three prompts a stranger would type. One broad ("best waffles in Waikiki"), one about your specialty and one that names your area in a different way.
2. Run each one in ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity and Google AI Mode.
3. Log the date and time, where you appear, who appears above you and which sites each tool links.
4. Look at the sources, not only the names. The links tell you which pages are doing the work.
5. Repeat it in a month. Answers move around, so one run is a snapshot.

Paid tools exist for this. Local Falcon, for instance, sells visibility tracking for ChatGPT mentions. A manual test is free and tells you most of what a first look needs, and I would start there.

## What should I do first?

Pick one specific thing you are best at and say it everywhere. That was the gap in my test. For the broad question, 'Aiwi lost to a shop with about 2,900 reviews. For gluten-free waffles, it won in every tool, and I don't think that is an accident. The site, the listings and the directories all say the same plain thing.

It matches what customers have told me about the site itself. They searched "breakfast spot Waikiki," saw the 'Aiwi site come up first, opened it next to about four other sites, and picked 'Aiwi because its site looked professional, inviting, warm and friendly while the others looked outdated or had copy written incorrectly. A chatbot and a hungry tourist are reading the same page.

Here is the order I'd go in:

1. Check your robots.txt for OAI-SearchBot and remove any rule that blocks it by accident.
2. Decide the one thing you want to be the answer for, in a sentence a stranger would understand.
3. Put that sentence on your homepage, in your Google Business Profile description and on each directory listing.
4. Claim your listings on Yelp, Bing Places and the directories for your niche, and make the details match.
5. Keep asking happy customers the honest way, by talking with them, never by pressure or by only asking the people who loved it.
6. Run the manual test now, and again in a month.

It's boring, and I know that. A lot of the guides on this topic want to sell you a dashboard. The thing that worked in my test is that the shop knows what it is, and so does everything on the internet that describes it.
