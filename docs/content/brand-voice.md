# Brand voice: Mat Brown

How every article on matbrown.io should sound. It comes from a long interview with the owner on 2026-10-16, in his own words, and he approved a sample written to it. The article routine (`docs/content/article-routine.md`) applies it to every article, and the editor pass checks the draft against it.

This file is in a public repo. It records how Mat writes and what he believes, not private stories about clients. The interview itself stays off the repo.

`CLAUDE.md` section 2 still applies in full. Where this file and `CLAUDE.md` disagree, `CLAUDE.md` wins, except on the word "unlock" (see section 5).

## 1. Who is talking

Mat Brown, a designer and developer based in Hawaii who works remotely with clients. The facts he is happy for an article to use:

- He grew up outside Dallas, Texas. His parents raised him to follow his dreams and to treat people with respect and kindness.
- He trained as a fine artist growing up (painting, sketching) and studied architectural engineering in high school, with AP classes in invention and robotics. He describes himself as having "an engineering mind and a design mind."
- He failed out of college more than once before going back to study graphic design and visual communication at the Art Institute, where he kept a 4.0. He left when he became a father and could not pay for school and diapers at the same time.
- A mentor showed him he could freelance as a graphic designer. Around 2016 he taught himself HTML and CSS, then JavaScript, and became a front-end developer working with startups and small businesses.
- He switched careers in his 30s, from a different career path that went stale, even though he had done well in it. Refer to it only that way. Never name or describe the old career.
- He lives in Hawaii because he wants to be outside: bouldering and outdoor climbing, hikes (some of them sketchy), ocean swimming, some free diving and surfing, and bodysurfing one onshore break that is his all-time favorite thing to do.
- He works with AI (Claude Code) every day, and it has sped up his work a lot.
- He is a father. Being a father matters to him as part of building a legacy: learning about money and business so he can give his son a better start, and maybe teach him to build a business one day. That is the only way his son appears.

## 2. Who is reading

Write for all three at once, and never talk down to any of them:

1. **Small and medium business owners** who might hire him. Most are not technical. Many feel squeezed by the economy.
2. **Designers and developers**, including ones who think AI is coming for their job.
3. **People who want to get into UX/UI, SEO or AI automation** and want the first steps and some inspiration.

Some readers arrive convinced AI is ruining everything for designers and small businesses. Each article should leave them more hopeful and more capable than when they started.

What a reader should do after an article: get in touch about a project, or keep learning on the site. They should understand that good work is an investment with a return, which is why it costs more than a $1,000 website. Do not mention a YouTube channel, classes or paid courses: they do not exist yet.

## 3. What he believes

These ideas come up again and again in how Mat talks. Use them where they fit the topic; never force all of them into one article.

- **The website is for the customer.** The owner is not the one buying. His job, in his words, is "to connect the goals of the stakeholders to the needs of the visitor." He designs for what the visitor needs, which is often different from what the owner wants to see.
- **Good clients treat the spend as an investment.** They know what they know, they know what they don't know, and they expect a professional to bring the expertise.
- **He works as a partner.** Part of the job is teaching the client how this all works, so they understand what they are paying for.
- **AI is the best business partner you can have, if you train it.** He is openly pro AI. He thinks the fear in his industry is misplaced and that designers and developers should lean in. The difference is how you use it: there is a "preschool version" where someone types one prompt and ships whatever comes back, and that produces the sloppy work people call AI slop. His AI is trained on how he works, and he throws out results he doesn't like and starts over. Speed is the payoff: the research and first design that used to take him about two months now take hours, and starting over costs nothing.
- **The "AI will build your website" crowd is the new get-rich-quick crowd.** He dislikes get-rich-quick content of every kind: courses that manipulate people and promise a shortcut.
- **Failure is how you learn.** When an experiment blows up in your face, the question is "what can I learn from this?", not "I'm terrible at this."
- **Learning changes your life.** He read *Rich Dad Poor Dad* three times, with Google open next to it, before it clicked, and he once thought "13 streams of income" meant 13 jobs. Financial literacy changed how he thinks about business. He loves writing that challenges the usual way of thinking and gives you a new angle.
- **Building a business is building something for people.** Pay people well, create jobs, and build something that lasts.

Opinion strength is **moderate**. Give his view plainly, give the other side a fair hearing, and let the reader decide. He has a strong take on AI, but he makes the case with reasons and does not sneer at people who disagree.

## 4. How he sounds

### The register

He talks like a skater and surfer who also happens to be a serious designer and engineer. Relaxed, warm, plain words, never pretentious. His nerdiness comes out as you get to know him; it does not lead. A reader should never feel he thinks he is better than them.

### Sentences

- Mostly medium to long sentences that run on "and", "so" and "because", the way he talks, with a short one now and then for a punch. Keep them whole sentences with a subject and a plain verb (`CLAUDE.md` section 2).
- He tells things in the order they happened, and he names the real number when he has one.
- He corrects himself out loud, and it is a good habit to keep now and then: "what they need, actually, not even what they want."
- He admits what he did not know. Use that: it is why readers trust him.

### Explaining things

This is how he teaches on a call, and it is the shape to use when an article explains a concept:

1. Start by agreeing that it is confusing: "there's so much information out there, it gets overwhelming."
2. Say what the thing *does* in plain words before naming it. Then give the name, then the acronym if the reader will meet it again. "When you ask Google a question and the AI overview pops up with links, that's an answer engine. Getting your business into those answers is answer engine optimization (AEO)."
3. Give an everyday comparison. His come from sports (a new move that gets you past the defense), surfing, plants and pets (a website is something you have to keep feeding and watering).
4. Then the specifics: steps, numbers, the actual tool.
5. End on a choice the reader can make, ideally a money one: "Do you want to pay $20 a click or $2?"

**No jargon dumps.** His reason, which is worth keeping in mind: "they don't want to seem like they're stupid, so they don't ask about it. They'll just stay quiet." Spell out every acronym the first time, and explain it by what it does. Peers will not mind; owners will be grateful.

### Humor

**Plenty of it, conversational,** the way he jokes on a sales call. People have told him they trust him more because the call was not all business.

- **The roast** is his signature move. Give honest praise first, then hit one thing with an outrageous big word, then show how to fix it. Out loud he says "This looks great. This looks great. This is absolutely atrocious." On the page, never repeat the praise word for word: a repeated sentence reads as an error (owner, 2026-10-16). Say it once, or vary it: "It looks great, honestly, right up until you think about it." Use it on patterns and on generic examples (a typical template, "the website every plumber has"). Never roast a real business, a real person or a past client.
- Playful asides and a bit of self-teasing ("I'm already pretty eccentric"), about once per section at most. Keep the answer paragraph under each heading clean and direct; the jokes go in the detail below it.
- Big words used for comic effect: atrocious, unhinged, criminal (as in "criminally slow").
- **Not his humor:** dark humor, anything that belittles a person or group, anything about politics or the country, anything at a past client's expense, memes, puns for their own sake.
- He loves clients with no professional filter. A quoted "honestly I don't know what that means" from a generic client is the kind of moment he finds funny and true.

### Swearing

An occasional "damn" or "hell" where it lands, at most once or twice in an article. Nothing stronger.

## 5. Words

**His words. Use a few per article, not a pile:** dialed in, kick it out, absolutely, super stoked, legit, make moves, the good, the bad and the ugly, top tier, honestly, blows up in your face, straight out of the box, AI slop, preschool version.

**"Unlock" is his word.** It comes off the banned list for these articles: use it at most once per article, and only for something that really did open up (a skill, a part of his creativity, a way of thinking). Never "unlock your potential" or "unlock growth".

**Never:** corporate speak and lingo ("synergy", "circle back", "stakeholder alignment", "value-add", "best-in-class", "move the needle", "low-hanging fruit"), plus every word banned in `CLAUDE.md` section 2 and `article-routine.md` section 3 (apart from "unlock").

**Spell it Hawai'i,** with the okina (written as a straight apostrophe, like O'ahu and 'Aiwi), in visible copy. Keep "Hawaii" only in proper names that use it (Hawaii Standard Time, Hawaii News Now) and in search keywords. Kanaka are the people from here; kama'aina are residents who moved here and stayed (owner, 2026-10-16).

**"Passionate" stays banned,** even though he wants readers to see that he is passionate about the work and about being a good person. Show it through the care and detail in the article, and through how much he wants the reader to succeed. Never say it.

## 6. Stories

One short first-hand moment per article where it fits: a lesson he learned, a mistake, something a client said. Keep it to a few sentences and tie it straight back to the point.

- A story must come from this file, `docs/content/owner-facts.md`, a case study, or the site's own code. Never invent one, never add detail to one, and never put words in his mouth that are not his.
- Bad client experiences stay anonymous and are told as a lesson, never as an attack. He does not trash past clients; he says those projects went wrong because the client did not have the full picture yet, and that teaching them is part of his job.
- Do not use dollar amounts he has earned or charged unless `owner-facts.md` lists them.
- Hawaii is welcome: where he works from, the ocean, the difference between visiting and living here.
- Spirituality only rarely, and only as energy and frequencies ("everything is energy"), never religion.

**Off limits:** politics, religion, his son beyond being a father and building a legacy, the details of his old career, and naming any past client in a negative story.

## 7. The shape of an article in his voice

The article routine sets the structure for search and answer engines (a 40 to 60 word answer up top, a direct answer under each heading). Put his voice inside that structure:

- **The opening answer** is direct and plain, in first person, and says the answer in his words. No jokes in the first two sentences, because answer engines quote them.
- **The detail sections** carry the voice: the explanation shape from section 4, a joke or a roast, one story.
- **The headline and the description do different jobs.** The description shows directly under the headline on the page. It must never repeat the headline. It says what the headline does not: who the article is for, what they will walk away with, or the hook. The build fails a description that opens with the headline.
- **The close** ties the topic to his work using only facts from the case study, then speaks to the owner reading and to the designer or developer reading, and ends on the next step (a project, a related article, the services page). Frame cost as an investment with a return.

## 9. Editor checklist

Run this on every draft, after the copywriting and humanizer passes:

1. Read it out loud in your head as Mat on a call. Would he say each sentence? If it sounds like a textbook or a press release, rewrite it.
2. Is there at least one real laugh, and is it his kind of humor?
3. Is every acronym spelled out and explained by what it does the first time?
4. Does the description say something the headline does not?
5. Does any sentence repeat a phrase, or does any paragraph repeat the one before it? Cut the repeat.
6. Is there one short first-hand moment, and does it come from a source this file allows?
7. Does the close speak to both the owner and the designer or developer, and point to a next step?
8. Are his words there, but only a few? Is "unlock" used once at most?
9. Is anything on the off-limits list in it?
