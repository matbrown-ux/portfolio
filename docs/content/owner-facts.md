## The career switch (stated 2026-10-16, My story interview; cleared for publishing)

- It took about 4 or 5 years before I actually knew what I was doing and how to do it. I wasn't able to live on the new work for probably the first 8 years of freelancing. You can do it a lot faster with more effort; I just didn't know what I was doing.
- While I still had the old career I did small freelance jobs. Most of my clients came from referrals then too, but slowly: about 1 or 2 a month, at maybe $100 to $300 each. It didn't seem viable.
- The overlap: the old job ran 3pm to 9pm. Some nights I did an hour or two of learning or work after 9. In the morning I did freelance design projects until I left for the old job at 2pm, then started over.
- After about 5 years I tried to go full time on freelance. It didn't work, and I went back to the old career.
- Then the old career blew up in my face: the stress, the issues and the people I couldn't stand any more. I walked away with no plan.
- A friend who was the general manager of two bars brought me on, though I had never worked in a bar. I worked there 10 months and barely made rent. I kept telling people I met there about my freelance skills and never got hired. Then I got tired of it, took the leap and bet on myself.
- I drove my car from Dallas to San Diego, shipped it to Hawaii in a container, took a one-way flight from San Diego, and I've been here ever since.
- In Hawaii I talked to people about business, digital marketing and their websites every single day. I got clients, but low-quality ones: people who said "$1,000 or $2,000 is a big investment for me" when they had never thought of it as an investment, only as an expense whose return they couldn't see. After about a year to a year and a half, that blew up in my face too.
- Then I helped 'Aiwi Waffles build the business from the inside: working with the owner on the food truck, building out the shop, scaling the business and building business systems. I essentially became an employee there. (Tell it warmly; the owner is a client I like working with.)
- I kept pitching UX/UI freelance work and it wasn't going anywhere, and I started to give up. Then I got connected with the CEO and founder of Vamp Network. That gave me the confidence to push other projects and find other clients, and that's how I run my own small freelance business now.
- My clients now still have smaller budgets, but they see me as a professional. They ask for my expertise instead of telling me what to do. I'm now in a position to go after bigger-budget clients and build a pipeline for them.
- I learned through online courses (Udemy, YouTube) and by building projects.
- What changed: I started paying attention to the conversations I had with clients, until I could tell whether someone was ready to invest, whether they were serious, and whether my price would scare them off. I learned a lot from Alex Hormozi, Tony Robbins, Dean Graziosi and Gary Vaynerchuk: several Tony Robbins books, and a lot of Alex Hormozi's content on social media and YouTube. It changed how I present myself and how I approach conversations with prospective clients. I'm still going through mindset shifts today.
- How it felt: like it wasn't going anywhere. Many times I felt I should give up and get a job somewhere else. My parents told me to keep a full-time job to pay for my living expenses and build the freelance business as I go, which was great advice.
- My advice: don't quit your job and go all in until you know what you're doing, and that takes a solid amount of education. Educate yourself while you're still in the old career and do small projects. Replace your old income by a factor of 2 or even 3 before you quit.

## Living in Hawaii and working remotely (stated 2026-10-16, My story interview; cleared for publishing)

- I start my day at about 8am by checking client messages and the Slack channel.
- The time zone is easy: I tell clients I'm in Hawaii. Sometimes they forget and text me at 4 in the morning, and they don't get upset when I reply later. If they forget, I remind them of the time difference.
- Lately I've been too busy to get in the water or climb. I plan to bring it back two or three times a week, in the morning, to start my day.
- I work from a home office, and sometimes from a friend's studio. I have my own fiber internet. I never have to fly anywhere for work.
- I find and keep clients mostly through referrals and the work itself, by keeping clients happy and giving them what they want. For Portfolio Optimization, Vamp Network's marketing events lead people to book video calls with me, I close them on the call, and we communicate through Slack. For other work I send people my portfolio link. I'm building a LinkedIn strategy to reach bigger-budget clients.
- I FaceTime my parents and friends on the mainland. When they ask me to come visit, I say: "Why would I come visit you there when you can come visit me here?"
- Visiting is the sights, the beaches, the touristy stuff, the expensive restaurants and not knowing how to get around. Living here, it's a small place and you end up knowing a lot of people. You learn the best hikes and beaches, when to go and how to stay away from the crowds. Expensive sushi is a treat once or twice a month, not a habit.
- People grow their own vegetables and spices and have mango and papaya trees. People buy local: raw honey from someone who keeps bees, co-ops for milk and grass-fed beef. If you're brave you can spearfish, but learn sustainable practice and don't overfish an area.
- Jobs favor people who aren't a flight risk. Apply without an 808 phone number or a Hawaii driver's license and you're probably not taken seriously.
- Advice before the one-way ticket: learn about Hawaii and the local community (how people talk and act) before you move; have a strategy and know what you're trying to do; housing is hard to find, amenities differ (air conditioning is not normal in homes); plan 6 to 8 months of rent and food, and be ready to struggle at first.
- Come with the mindset that you will always be a visitor. You can call Hawaii home, but you won't be "from here" or "local." In my experience most people who move get island fever and leave after about a year; make it past one and you'll probably make 3, then 5 and 7, and past 7 it's home.
- Show up as a visitor: leave beaches and hikes cleaner than you found them, even other people's trash. Volunteer, for example on a lo'i (taro patch), and learn about Polynesian culture. Learn a few Pidgin sayings; you'll sound silly at first and get teased, so laugh at it, and locals respect the effort. How you greet people and throw a shaka shows whether you live here. Follow Hawaii Instagram accounts to learn how people are before you arrive.
- Accuracy (owner approved 2026-10-16): write Pidgin as Hawaii Creole English, its own language and not the Hawaiian language; a lo'i is a taro patch; spell Hawai'i with the okina.
- Correction from the owner (2026-10-16): Kanaka roughly translates to "the people" and means the people from here. Kama'aina means roughly "people of the land" and is used for residents only: people who moved here and stayed. Do not use the dictionary sense "child of the land" or "people born here" for kama'aina.

## Creator portfolio starter (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- I built the first creator portfolio sites one at a time from scratch. Then I had Claude Code help me make a scaffolding template, because they all had parts that stayed the same.
- What stays the same: the tech stack, the test-driven development kit I built (every site uses it), the flow of the home page, and some similar components.
- What changes for each creator: their own custom colors and logo; fonts they choose, free ones from Google Fonts or another open-license source; copy written for them, usually from a brand voice document they share with me. Page sections usually stay the same, but a few creators have asked me to switch things up, and I do it.
- I built a skill for the starter: I call a slash command with the creator's name and their previous portfolio URL, and it scaffolds the whole codebase and pulls in the information from their old portfolio. Then I go through a design phase where I tell Claude how I want everything designed and give it reference images: color palettes creators find on Pinterest, and typography examples they like. We find the closest font, and if the one they want has an open license, we use it.
- Every creator's portfolio is completely custom to what they want it to look like.
- Time: the starter cut out a lot of the first part of the build. I wouldn't say it saved time overall, but it made projects easier to start and gets me to the design phase sooner. Before, I wrote long, highly detailed prompts I had to sit and think about, usually with the same design skill requests and CLAUDE.md rules. The starter covers all of that. It shaved off a few hours per site, for sure.

## Automation pricing (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- There are no set prices for automation work; it is custom to each client. Monthly upkeep is on a retainer.
- I have a conversation with the client. When they're ready, we build out a scope, and the scope sets the price, all on the same call.
- Once they say yes, it is a monthly retainer. I write an initial brief that sets out the build, the scope, which monthly upkeep tasks I'll do and how often. Retainers run from $2,000 to $10,000 a month, depending on the company and the size of the scope.
- Smaller jobs: usually a simple n8n setup or a scheduled task that saves the company a few hours a week. The upkeep is keeping that task relevant to what the business is doing at the time. The price goes up as more workflows are added.
- Bigger jobs: usually need a research library (a knowledge base) for the automation to use. Example (hypothetical, not a client): for a law firm, a library built on proper practice for its kind of law and the regulations and compliance rules it has to follow; the monthly upkeep is maintaining that library and pruning anything outdated as laws and regulations change.
- Bigger jobs also mean working out what employees do now and which parts of their jobs can be automated. Automations don't need to replace employees; they let people work faster, get more done in a day and not burn out on menial, monotonous tasks.

## Designing without Figma (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- I don't design in Figma and bring it into Claude Code with the Figma MCP server. I have a different flow that works really well.
- Without direction from the client, I search Pinterest (for example, "luxury web design"), make a mood board and download the images into a reference folder. For dashboards I pull dashboards and screen flows from Mobbin (mobbin.com) into the reference folder.
- The references are a starting point so I'm not sitting in Figma placing things by hand. By the time the design is production ready it has changed completely from the references; I keep certain layouts and ideas, the way I always used inspiration.
- Instead of dragging out frames and building wireframes and user flows by hand, I prompt Claude to restructure parts of the site the way I want. More of it happens while I'm in a creative flow, and the design doesn't go stale.
- When a client needs wireframes or user flow screens, I make them with Claude, bring in the client's revision requests, rework them and send a version 2. Once they approve, I design and build from those wireframes.
- It's much easier to set up a design system once you know the direction of the build than before there is any wireframing or understanding of the site's architecture.

## Logo and brand pricing (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- A basic logo is usually $1,000 to $1,500. The client gets 5 to 7 first concepts, which usually narrow to 1 or 2 once they say what they like, and up to 3 rounds of revisions (a basic logo usually takes one). The final is one logo in large, medium and small formats.
- Large: a word mark with the logotype and an icon. Medium: a more basic lockup. Small: the icon in a badge, or the icon alone, for a profile image or packaging.
- I deliver a multi-page PDF plus the Adobe Illustrator vector file.
- A full brand identity system adds brand voice documents and brand guidelines: a brand bible the client's marketing team or future designers can work from. Bigger packages add packaging and merch mockups (T-shirts, hats, mugs, bags), every logo file they need (Facebook and Instagram profiles, social media banners), starter carousels for social campaigns, and layered Illustrator or Photoshop templates for different setups.
- Full brand packages have gone up to $6,000 and $7,000, and can go higher, because some brands need a lot more.

## n8n, Zapier and Make (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- I have not used Zapier or Make on a real project. I know how both work, but I haven't needed either.
- I chose n8n for its flexibility and how customizable it is. When I started, it was extremely hard to learn because of all the customization, and it got overwhelming.
- Once Claude Code did the setup and dialed everything in for me, it got a lot easier, and managing the workflows made sense because I understood how they worked instead of worrying about what goes where.
- My advice: don't build n8n workflows on your own. Hook it up to Claude Code, have it structure the whole workflow from your goals, and import the file into n8n. You'll tweak some things as you go, but for the most part it just works.

## Custom software pricing (stated 2026-10-16, needs-input interview; cleared for publishing)

- For dashboards and internal tools, my minimum budget is $10,000, going up with the scope. That is usually a one-time build fee.
- Usually the client has no in-house developer or designer to maintain the code, so instead of calling me back for each fix they pay a monthly retainer for upkeep and any changes they ask for: $2,500 to $5,000 a month.
- If I do the upkeep, I host on my own Netlify account so DNS changes are easier for me. If the client has in-house people who will manage it, I deploy to their own Netlify account or whatever hosting they want. Either way, every other account the build needs (the database, usually Supabase, and any API accounts) is in the client's own name, so they own the whole stack and the intellectual property. I set those accounts up and manage them, as an authorized user or with logins the client gives me.
- Software that lives in my accounts wouldn't really be theirs, so I've never set it up that way.
