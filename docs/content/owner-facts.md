## The career switch (stated 2026-10-16, My story interview; cleared for publishing)

- It took about 4 or 5 years before I actually knew what I was doing and how to do it. I wasn't able to live on the new work for probably the first 8 years of freelancing. You can do it a lot faster with more effort; I just didn't know what I was doing.
- While I still had the old career I did small freelance jobs. Most of my clients came from referrals then too, but slowly: about 1 or 2 a month, at maybe $100 to $300 each. It didn't seem viable.
- The overlap: the old job ran 3pm to 9pm. Some nights I did an hour or two of learning or work after 9. In the morning I did freelance design projects until I left for the old job at 2pm, then started over.
- After about 5 years I tried to go full time on freelance. It didn't work, and I went back to the old career.
- Then the old career blew up in my face: the stress, the issues and the people I couldn't stand any more. I walked away with no plan.
- A friend who was the general manager of two bars brought me on, though I had never worked in a bar. I worked there 10 months and barely made rent. I kept telling people I met there about my freelance skills and never got hired. Then I got tired of it, took the leap and bet on myself.
- I drove my car from Dallas to San Diego, shipped it to Hawaii in a container, took a one-way flight from San Diego, and I've been here ever since.
- In Hawaii I talked to people about business, digital marketing and their websites every single day. I got clients, but low-quality ones: people who said "$1,000 or $2,000 is a big investment for me" when they had never thought of it as an investment, only as an expense whose return they couldn't see. After about a year to a year and a half, that blew up in my face too.
- Then I helped 'Aiwi Waffles build the business from the inside: working with the owner on the food truck, building out the shop, scaling the business and building business systems. I essentially became an employee there. (Tell it warmly; the owner is a client I like working with.)
- I kept pitching UX/UI freelance work and it wasn't going anywhere, and I started to give up. Then I got connected with the CEO and founder of Vamp Network. That gave me the confidence to push other projects and find other clients, and that's how I run my own small freelance business now.
- My clients now still have smaller budgets, but they see me as a professional. They ask for my expertise instead of telling me what to do. I'm now in a position to go after bigger-budget clients and build a pipeline for them.
- I learned through online courses (Udemy, YouTube) and by building projects.
- What changed: I started paying attention to the conversations I had with clients, until I could tell whether someone was ready to invest, whether they were serious, and whether my price would scare them off. I learned a lot from Alex Hormozi, Tony Robbins, Dean Graziosi and Gary Vaynerchuk: several Tony Robbins books, and a lot of Alex Hormozi's content on social media and YouTube. It changed how I present myself and how I approach conversations with prospective clients. I'm still going through mindset shifts today.
- How it felt: like it wasn't going anywhere. Many times I felt I should give up and get a job somewhere else. My parents told me to keep a full-time job to pay for my living expenses and build the freelance business as I go, which was great advice.
- My advice: don't quit your job and go all in until you know what you're doing, and that takes a solid amount of education. Educate yourself while you're still in the old career and do small projects. Replace your old income by a factor of 2 or even 3 before you quit.

## Living in Hawaii and working remotely (stated 2026-10-16, My story interview; cleared for publishing)

- I start my day at about 8am by checking client messages and the Slack channel.
- The time zone is easy: I tell clients I'm in Hawaii. Sometimes they forget and text me at 4 in the morning, and they don't get upset when I reply later. If they forget, I remind them of the time difference.
- Lately I've been too busy to get in the water or climb. I plan to bring it back two or three times a week, in the morning, to start my day.
- I work from a home office, and sometimes from a friend's studio. I have my own fiber internet. I never have to fly anywhere for work.
- I find and keep clients mostly through referrals and the work itself, by keeping clients happy and giving them what they want. For Portfolio Optimization, Vamp Network's marketing events lead people to book video calls with me, I close them on the call, and we communicate through Slack. For other work I send people my portfolio link. I'm building a LinkedIn strategy to reach bigger-budget clients.
- I FaceTime my parents and friends on the mainland. When they ask me to come visit, I say: "Why would I come visit you there when you can come visit me here?"
- Visiting is the sights, the beaches, the touristy stuff, the expensive restaurants and not knowing how to get around. Living here, it's a small place and you end up knowing a lot of people. You learn the best hikes and beaches, when to go and how to stay away from the crowds. Expensive sushi is a treat once or twice a month, not a habit.
- People grow their own vegetables and spices and have mango and papaya trees. People buy local: raw honey from someone who keeps bees, co-ops for milk and grass-fed beef. If you're brave you can spearfish, but learn sustainable practice and don't overfish an area.
- Jobs favor people who aren't a flight risk. Apply without an 808 phone number or a Hawaii driver's license and you're probably not taken seriously.
- Advice before the one-way ticket: learn about Hawaii and the local community (how people talk and act) before you move; have a strategy and know what you're trying to do; housing is hard to find, amenities differ (air conditioning is not normal in homes); plan 6 to 8 months of rent and food, and be ready to struggle at first.
- Come with the mindset that you will always be a visitor. You can call Hawaii home, but you won't be "from here" or "local." In my experience most people who move get island fever and leave after about a year; make it past one and you'll probably make 3, then 5 and 7, and past 7 it's home.
- Show up as a visitor: leave beaches and hikes cleaner than you found them, even other people's trash. Volunteer, for example on a lo'i (taro patch), and learn about Polynesian culture. Learn a few Pidgin sayings; you'll sound silly at first and get teased, so laugh at it, and locals respect the effort. How you greet people and throw a shaka shows whether you live here. Follow Hawaii Instagram accounts to learn how people are before you arrive.
- Accuracy (owner approved 2026-10-16): write Pidgin as Hawaii Creole English, its own language and not the Hawaiian language; a lo'i is a taro patch; "local" means raised in Hawaii, and kama'aina (literally "child of the land") is used for Hawaii residents, especially those born here; cite sources.
